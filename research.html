Research

Five strands, one question: who benefits, and how do we know?

Most of my work asks who gains from a treatment or a policy, by how much, and what evidence would let us say so honestly. I develop causal inference and machine learning methods for that purpose, and I use them to evaluate insurance reforms, provider payment, digital care and the health consequences of conflict, in the UK, the US and low- and middle-income countries.

01

Causal inference for comparative effectiveness and economic evaluation

Health economic evaluation leans heavily on observational data, and the people who receive a treatment rarely resemble those who do not. My earliest work asked which estimators cope best with that problem. Through simulations and applied case studies, I showed that semiparametric methods such as targeted maximum likelihood estimation and bias-corrected matching beat standard propensity score approaches on both bias and efficiency once the model is misspecified.

I then carried these tools into longitudinal settings with time-varying confounding, evaluating dynamic treatment regimes in critical care, and into electronic health records where biomarkers are measured only when a clinician decides to measure them. The aim throughout has been practical: to make adaptive treatment strategies evaluable with the data health systems actually collect.

  • Kreif et al. (2017), American Journal of Epidemiology

02

Causal machine learning and policy learning

Average treatment effects hide a great deal. A policy that does nothing on average may help one group and harm another, and the value of a programme often sits in that heterogeneity. I have brought causal forests, meta-learners and policy learning into health economics and outcomes research, applying them to Indonesia's national health insurance programme and to the question of how individualised treatment effect estimates should feed into cost-effectiveness decision models.

On the methods side, my MRC New Investigator programme with James Cussens produced policy learning for rare outcomes, fast algorithms for optimal policy trees, and machine learning for staggered difference-in-differences. I co-authored the ISPOR PALISADE checklist for machine learning in health economics and outcomes research, and co-led a review of individualised treatment effect methods for use in health technology assessment.

03

Quasi-experimental evaluation of health policies

National policies rarely arrive with a control group. When a payment reform or an incentive scheme is rolled out across a whole system, the counterfactual has to be built from what happened elsewhere. Using data from the English NHS, I examined how the synthetic control method behaves when there are many treated units and set it against difference-in-differences and related designs. The point was to show how much a causal conclusion can depend on that choice.

These designs have since served me in evaluations of hospital payment reforms and provider incentive programmes in England. More recently I have co-led an evaluation of digital-first primary care, asking who gained access and who lost it when consultations moved online, and what that means for the trade-off between equity and efficiency.

04

Health systems in low- and middle-income countries

Health systems in low- and middle-income countries make consequential decisions on thin evidence, and the same causal tools apply there. In South Africa, colleagues and I asked whether national facility quality standards improve care uniformly, or only for clinics already above a baseline level of quality. The answer matters for how quality incentives should be designed and targeted.

In Indonesia, I produced estimates of the productivity of the health system that now underpin the supply-side cost-effectiveness threshold used in reimbursement decisions. Related work with the same team extended to Malawi. Much of this began under the NIHR-funded GHE2 programme at the University of York and continues through the NIHR Global Health Research Unit on health financing for universal health coverage.

05

Conflict, violence, mental health and economic outcomes

Conflict, violence and mental ill-health leave marks on economic life that are hard to measure, because exposure is never random. This strand brings the same care about confounding and selection to those settings. With Rodrigo Moreno-Serra and colleagues at York, I studied how armed conflict in Colombia affected child health and how it reshaped the financing of the health system.

In the United States, we estimated the effect of psychological distress in adolescence on health and economic outcomes in adulthood. In Brazil, the BRAVE project traced the relationship between pandemic distancing policies and violence against women. Each of these projects pairs a substantive question with a design that can actually answer it. My current MQ Foundation Fellowship in Economics and Mental Health Research extends this work on the economics of mental health.

People

Collaborators and visiting positions

Much of my methods work grew out of time spent at the University of California, Berkeley: as a visiting graduate student in 2010, as a visiting researcher in the Division of Biostatistics in 2015, and again in 2022 at the Center for Targeted Machine Learning and Causal Inference. In 2016 I was a visiting researcher at the Kaiser Permanente Division of Research. Since 2019 I have been an Honorary Research Fellow in Health Economics and Health Technology Assessment (HEHTA) at the University of Glasgow.

I work with economists, biostatisticians, epidemiologists and clinicians across the University of Washington, York, the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine, Imperial College London and Harvard, and with partners in Indonesia, Colombia, Brazil, South Africa and Malawi. If any of this overlaps with what you do, I would be glad to hear from you.
