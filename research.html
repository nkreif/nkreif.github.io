Research

Research

My research develops and applies causal inference and machine learning methods for evaluating health policies and treatments, with a focus on how effects vary across people. This page describes the five main strands of that work, the projects that supported it, and the people I have worked with.

01

Causal inference for comparative effectiveness and economic evaluation

Economic evaluations often rely on observational data, in which treated and untreated patients differ in ways that affect outcomes. An early strand of my work compared estimators for this setting. Using simulations and applied case studies, we found that semiparametric methods such as targeted maximum likelihood estimation and bias-corrected matching performed better than standard propensity score approaches, in terms of bias and efficiency, when the models were misspecified.

Later work extended these methods to longitudinal settings with time-varying confounding, including the evaluation of dynamic treatment regimes in critical care, and to electronic health record data in which biomarkers are measured only when clinicians decide to measure them. The common aim has been to make adaptive treatment strategies evaluable with the data that health systems routinely collect.

02

Causal machine learning and policy learning

A policy with a small average effect may help some groups and not others. This strand applies causal machine learning methods, including causal forests, meta-learners and policy learning, to estimate heterogeneous treatment effects in health policy and clinical settings. Applications include Indonesia's national health insurance program and the question of how individualized treatment effect estimates can be used within cost-effectiveness decision models.

On the methods side, an MRC New Investigator Research Grant with James Cussens supported work on policy learning with rare outcomes, algorithms for learning optimal policy trees, and machine learning for staggered difference-in-differences designs. I was a member of the ISPOR task force that produced the PALISADE checklist for machine learning in health economics and outcomes research, and co-led a review of methods for estimating individualized treatment effects for use in health technology assessment.

03

Quasi-experimental evaluation of health policies

When a payment reform or an incentive scheme is introduced across a whole health system, the counterfactual has to be constructed from comparison units. Using data from the English NHS, we examined how the synthetic control method behaves when there are multiple treated units, and compared it with difference-in-differences and related designs, to show how conclusions can depend on the choice of method.

These designs have been used in evaluations of hospital payment reforms and provider incentive programs in England. More recent work, with colleagues at Imperial College London, evaluates digital-first models of primary care and the trade-off between equity and efficiency in access.

04

Health systems in low- and middle-income countries

This strand applies causal methods to questions of health system performance in low- and middle-income countries, including quality improvement policies, health expenditure expansions and provider payment reforms. In South Africa, we examined whether national facility quality standards improved care uniformly, or only for clinics already above a baseline level of quality, which has implications for how quality incentives are designed and targeted.

In Indonesia, we estimated the productivity of the health system, and these estimates have been used in supply-side cost-effectiveness threshold estimates for reimbursement decisions. Related work covers maternal health care in Malawi. Much of this research was carried out under the NIHR-funded GHE2 program at the University of York and the NIHR Global Health Research Unit on health financing for universal health coverage.

05

Conflict, violence, mental health and economic outcomes

Exposure to conflict, violence and mental ill-health is not random, which makes their consequences hard to measure. This strand applies causal inference methods to these questions. With Rodrigo Moreno-Serra and colleagues at York, we studied the effects of armed conflict in Colombia on child health and on the financing of the health system.

In the United States, we estimated the effect of psychological distress in adolescence on health and economic outcomes in adulthood. In Brazil, the BRAVE project examined the relationship between pandemic distancing policies and violence against women. My current MQ Foundation Fellowship in Economics and Mental Health Research continues this work on the economics of mental health.

Collaborators and visiting positions

Much of my methods work has connections to the University of California, Berkeley, where I was a visiting graduate student in 2010, a visiting researcher in the Division of Biostatistics in 2015, and a visiting researcher at the Center for Targeted Machine Learning and Causal Inference in 2022. In 2016 I was a visiting researcher at the Kaiser Permanente Division of Research.

I work with economists, biostatisticians, epidemiologists and clinicians at the University of Washington, the University of York, the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine, Imperial College London and Harvard, and with partners in Indonesia, Colombia, Brazil, South Africa and Malawi.
