About

Background

I am an Assistant Professor of Health Economics at the CHOICE Institute, University of Washington School of Pharmacy, which I joined in March 2024. I develop and apply causal inference and machine learning methods to estimate how the effects of health policies and treatments vary across people, and use these estimates to inform targeting, personalization and policy design. I have applied them to health insurance reforms, provider payment policies, digital health, mental health, and the health effects of conflict and violence, in high-income and low- and middle-income countries.

Before moving to Seattle I was a Senior Research Fellow at the Centre for Health Economics, University of York, and before that a Lecturer and MRC Early Career Fellow at the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine, where I completed my PhD in Health Economics in 2013.

Featured project

Tailoring health policies

MRC New Investigator Research Grant, 2020–2023 (MR/T04487X/1). Principal investigator, with James Cussens.

This project asked how causal machine learning can show who benefits most from a health policy, and how those estimates can be used to decide who should be eligible, for example to maximize total health benefit within a fixed budget or to take account of equity. We extended causal machine learning and policy learning methods to the settings that arise in health policy evaluation: confounding in non-experimental designs, costs as well as benefits, and budget constraints.

Long (about 190 words)

Noémi Kreif is an Assistant Professor of Health Economics at the CHOICE Institute, University of Washington School of Pharmacy. Her research develops causal inference and machine learning methods for evaluating health policies and treatments. She uses them to study how effects vary, for example which patients or households benefit most from a treatment or an insurance program, and how such evidence can inform targeting and policy design.

She has applied these methods to national health insurance reforms, provider payment policies, digital primary care, mental health and economic outcomes, and the health effects of conflict and violence, in high-income and low- and middle-income countries. She was a member of the ISPOR task force that produced the PALISADE checklist on machine learning in health economics and outcomes research, and she leads the UW graduate course on machine learning in health economics and outcomes research.

Before joining the University of Washington in 2024, she was a Senior Research Fellow at the Centre for Health Economics, University of York, and a Lecturer and MRC Early Career Fellow at the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine, where she received her PhD in Health Economics in 2013.
